\section{Introduction}
\rhostart{R}ainfall in Singapore is convective, highly localised and fast moving, so a station can pass from dry to heavy rain within a few five-minute intervals. This project asks a question an operational user would ask: given everything observed at a station up to the present moment, what is the probability that the next thirty minutes will accumulate more than a given amount of rain?

We treat this as a probabilistic classification problem over eight years of national observations, built end to end on Apache Spark~\cite{Lecture4}. The work has two halves. The first is an exploratory study of the observation network itself: how complete it is, how rainfall is distributed across months and hours, how those patterns change from 2017 to 2024, and which apparent extremes survive scrutiny. The second is a supervised forecasting experiment that compares three MLlib model families against two frequency baselines, selects a model chronologically, and then measures it once on held-out years.

Our main findings are that rainfall varies strongly from year to year without a consistent trend, with 2019 markedly dry because it had fewer wet days; that gradient-boosted trees reduce the macro Brier score by 40.92\,\% relative to a frequency baseline on the held-out years 2023--2024; and that adding the station's current and recent rainfall to contextual features supplies most of the measured improvement. Evaluation is strictly chronological throughout. Reported values were checked against the preserved outputs of the reported run, supplemented by a separate exact-AUC evaluation of its saved model.

\section{Data and Computing Environment}

\subsection{Data Source and Cleaning}
We use the NEA historical rainfall collection published through data.gov.sg~\cite{NEARainfall}, covering 2017 through 2024. Each record carries an observation timestamp, a station identifier, station metadata, coordinates, and a five-minute rainfall total in millimetres. The publisher states that timestamps are Singapore Time, that records may be missing, and that the series has not undergone climate-data quality control. We therefore treat the data as observations, not as a validated climate product.

All processing uses Spark DataFrames~\cite{Lecture4,Lecture5}. Exact semantic duplicates are removed. Where two records disagree for the same station and timestamp, both are excluded rather than arbitrarily resolved. Negative, non-finite, unparseable, geographically invalid and off-grid observations are excluded. Early timestamps falling within one second of the five-minute grid are aligned and audited rather than discarded. Table~\ref{tab:audit} gives the resulting record counts by year.

Two cleaning decisions matter for interpretation. Missing observations are never treated as zero rainfall, because an absent reading and a dry reading are different claims. And five-minute values above 50\,mm are retained and flagged rather than removed: that threshold is an engineering audit rule, not a meteorological limit, so silently deleting such values would discard precisely the extremes the study is interested in.

\section{Prediction Task and Protocol}
\label{sec:task}

The model answers the query posed by the task. Given a station identifier, a prediction time and a rainfall threshold, it returns the probability that the station accumulates more than that threshold over the following thirty minutes. The station's observations up to the prediction time are retrieved internally to build the features, so the caller supplies only these three inputs. For example,
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\texttt{python rainfall.py predict -{}-station S77}\\
\texttt{-{}-time 2024-01-01T01:00:00+08:00}\\
\texttt{-{}-threshold 5}
\end{center}
returns a probability of 0.019 that station S77 records more than 5\,mm between 01:00 and 01:30 SGT on 1 January 2024.

\subsection{Target Definition}
For station $s$ and prediction origin $t$, the target is the total rainfall over the next thirty minutes,
\[R_{30}(s,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{6}r(s,t+5k\text{ min}),\]
\[y(s,t,a)=\mathbf{1}[R_{30}(s,t)>a],\]
with strict exceedance of threshold $a$. Four thresholds are used: 0, 1, 5 and 10\,mm. These are experimental settings, not official warning levels.

One caveat concerns the timestamps. The published reading type confirms a five-minute total, but the collection does not state whether a timestamp marks the start or the end of its accumulation interval. The target is therefore defined over the next six \emph{recorded} observations; the physical interval-end reading is an assumption we cannot verify from the data.

\subsection{Splits and Sampling}
Training uses 2017--2021, validation uses 2022, and testing uses 2023--2024. Targets that would cross a split boundary are excluded, so no example is partly built from a neighbouring period. A complete example requires twelve past readings from $t-55$ to $t$ and six future readings from $t+5$ to $t+30$. No future observation enters any feature. Encoders, standardisation and baseline probabilities are fitted on training data only.

Of 48\,538\,625 cleaned readings, 47\,048\,853 yield a complete window; window completeness therefore excludes 3.07\,\% of candidate origins.

To keep local computation tractable, training origins are taken every thirty minutes and then sampled uniformly at 10\,\% using a stable hash of station and timestamp with seed 42. The sampling is deliberately origin-level rather than example-level: all four threshold examples derived from a retained origin stay together, so the threshold feature is never correlated with sampling. Because training origins are first taken at 30-minute intervals, the retained sample is about one sixtieth of all eligible five-minute training origins. Validation and testing retain every complete origin at 30-minute intervals, and the additional test in Section~\ref{sec:testperf} uses every complete five-minute origin. No class-dependent sampling or reweighting is applied anywhere. Table~\ref{tab:splits} gives the resulting example counts.

A single joint binary classifier is trained with the threshold as a numeric feature, rather than four separate models. This keeps one consistent decision surface and allows other non-negative thresholds to be queried, at the cost that threshold monotonicity is not structurally guaranteed. We measure that property rather than enforcing it.

Three MLlib families are compared~\cite{Lecture6,SparkMLlib}: logistic regression at regularisation 0.01, 0.1 and 1; random forest at 50 and 100 trees with depth 5 and 8; and gradient-boosted trees at 30 and 60 iterations with depth 3 and 5. That is eleven candidates, all sharing identical fitted F2 preprocessing.

\section{Experiment Design}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Selection Rule}
Candidates are ranked by validation macro Brier score, averaging the four thresholds equally. Selection uses 2022 only. The test years are never consulted during tuning, and no random cross-validation is used, because shuffling would leak information across time in a series with strong temporal dependence.

Once a winner is chosen, its family and hyperparameters are frozen for the F0/F1/F2 ablation, so that the ablation measures the contribution of features alone rather than a confounded change of model.

\subsection{Baselines}
Two baselines establish what a model must beat to be worth anything. B0 predicts the training exceedance frequency for each threshold, ignoring all inputs. B1 uses training station, month and threshold frequency, falling back to B0 for cells absent from training. B1 is the more demanding comparison: it already encodes the seasonal and spatial structure visible in the exploratory analysis.

\subsection{Evaluation}
The primary metric is the Brier score, which rewards calibrated probabilities rather than confident guesses. Log loss, PR-AUC and ROC-AUC are reported alongside it. PR-AUC is given particular weight at the higher thresholds, where exceedance is rare and ROC-AUC can conceal a high false-alarm burden. Reliability is assessed with ten equal-width probability bins.

For classification metrics, a per-threshold probability cutoff is chosen by maximising validation F1 over 0.05 to 0.95, and then held fixed for the test set.

\paragraph{No consistent trend.} From 2020 to 2024 the mean rainfall per qualifying station-day stays between 7.44 and 8.06\,mm with no consistent direction (Table~\ref{tab:yearly}; Figure~\ref{fig:annualmean} shows the station-averaged series). Eight years are in any case too short to separate a trend from ordinary year-to-year variability.

\paragraph{A dry 2019.} 2019 was markedly dry, through fewer wet days rather than lighter rain. Its mean of 4.69\,mm per station-day is about 39\,\% below the 2020--2024 average of 7.74\,mm. The share of wet station-days fell to 39.8\,\%, against 52.8--60.5\,\% in every other year (Table~\ref{tab:yearly}; Figure~\ref{fig:wetdays} shows the station-averaged fraction), while the intensity of the rain that did fall, 0.85\,mm per wet five-minute interval, was typical. The deficit was one of frequency, not intensity.

\paragraph{Heavier rain in 2021--2022.} These two years brought the heaviest rain. They have the highest wet-interval intensities, 0.91 and 0.90\,mm, the largest 99th-percentile station-day total (82.2\,mm in 2021) and the largest single five-minute reading (37.2\,mm in 2022). Their mean daily rainfall is close to that of neighbouring years, so the rain was heavier when it fell rather than more frequent.

\paragraph{A shifting seasonal cycle.} The seasonal cycle itself changes between years (Figure~\ref{fig:monthly}). In six of the eight years, the November, December and January months of the calendar year are wetter than June to August, consistent with the November peak above. In 2020 and 2021 the order reverses: June to August averages 8.58 and 8.91\,mm per station-day, against 7.67 and 8.11\,mm for November to January. The single wettest month falls in six different months across the eight years, and August 2018 averaged only 0.54\,mm per station-day. The November peak in the pooled data is therefore an average over years whose individual cycles differ considerably.

\paragraph{Changing network.} The number of stations with qualifying days rises from 54 in 2019 to 80 in 2020. To check that the yearly differences are not an artefact of a changing network, we repeated the calculation on the 37-station common-identifier panel. It tracks the all-station series to within 0.26\,mm per station-day in every year and reproduces the 2019 anomaly, at 4.67\,mm and 39.8\,\% wet days, so the conclusions above are not driven by network growth. They describe what was observed; these data alone cannot attribute the differences to particular climate drivers.

The selected model improves on B0 by 40.92\,\% (Table~\ref{tab:test}). B1 gives the more informative result: adding station and month structure to the baseline does \emph{not} improve the Brier score, scoring marginally worse than B0 at 0.02476 against 0.02472. Station and month frequencies alone therefore add little to a thirty-minute probability forecast, although this does not show that they carry no information at all. The ablation below shows that adding recent rainfall to the contextual features supplies most of the improvement.

Table~\ref{tab:perthreshold} breaks the selected model down by threshold. Brier falls with the threshold simply because exceedance becomes rarer, while PR-AUC falls too, from 0.679 at 0\,mm to 0.250 at 10\,mm. ROC-AUC, by contrast, stays between 0.890 and 0.910 at every threshold, which is why PR-AUC is the more informative guide for rare events. Heavy rain is therefore much harder to anticipate than the macro score alone suggests.

An additional evaluation retains every complete five-minute origin rather than only origins at 30-minute intervals, giving 13\,025\,429 origins per threshold and a macro Brier of 0.01460. The two scores agree to four decimal places, so the thirty-minute evaluation stride does not flatter the result. The two test years also behave similarly: at 0\,mm the Brier score is 0.03306 in 2023 and 0.03259 in 2024. Figures~\ref{fig:calib30} and~\ref{fig:calib5} show the corresponding reliability curves.

Ranking skill and calibration tell different stories at 10\,mm. The lowest probability bin holds 2\,153\,507 of the 2\,170\,886 test origins (99.2\,\%). In that bin the mean predicted probability is 1.98\,\% while the observed frequency is 0.19\,\%, so predictions in the bin overestimate the observed frequency on average, by a factor of about ten. The aggregate metrics also show a calibration weakness: the 10\,mm log loss of 0.0309 is about 19\,\% worse than B0's 0.0260, even though the Brier score improves by about 10\,\%. The model therefore ranks heavy-rain risk well, but its 10\,mm probabilities are not well calibrated in the low range where most origins lie. The sharp drop of the 10\,mm curve near a predicted probability of 0.76 comes from a bin holding a single origin and should not be read as a trend.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{What the Results Support}
Three findings are supported by the evidence and are robust to how the evaluation is sliced.

First, short-horizon rainfall exceedance is dominated by persistence. The ablation shows this clearly: of the measured Brier improvement from F0 to F2, 98\,\% comes from adding current rainfall and its lags, before any rolling summary is computed. This is consistent with B1 not improving on B0: seasonal and spatial frequency, which looks informative in the exploratory analysis, adds little to a thirty-minute Brier score on its own.

Second, the gain over a frequency baseline is real but bounded. A 40.9\,\% reduction in Brier score is substantial, yet at the 10\,mm threshold precision is 0.373 and PR-AUC is 0.250. The model is useful for ranking risk, not for issuing categorical warnings about heavy rain, and at 10\,mm its predictions overestimate the observed frequency on average in the lowest probability bin, which covers 99.2\,\% of origins (Section~\ref{sec:testperf}).

Third, the headline number is not an artefact of the evaluation stride. Re-running on all 13 million five-minute origins moves the macro Brier by 0.00001.

\subsection{What the Results Do Not Support}
The study cannot speak to long-term climate trends. Eight years support descriptive comparison only, and because no station achieves full-year coverage in all eight years, observed annual totals are incomplete by construction.

Nor can it claim operational readiness. Predictions assume every observation through time $t$ is already available; publication latency in the real feed was not measured. A deployment that receives data late would face a harder problem than the one evaluated here.

Finally, the extremes in Table~\ref{tab:extremes} are observations, not validated records. The 786.6\,mm station-day is reported because suppressing it would be worse, not because we are asserting it occurred.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt, itemsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{Completeness bias.} Missingness and the 95\,\% coverage filter can bias both the exploratory statistics and the evaluation sample, in directions that cannot be identified from these data alone.
\item \textbf{Unvalidated extremes.} Positive outliers received an audit rule, not external quality control.
\item \textbf{Station identity.} One station reports multiple coordinate pairs; a fixed identifier does not guarantee a fixed location.
\item \textbf{Training sample.} Uniform 10\,\% origin sampling controls computation but discards rare training events, which plausibly costs most at the 10\,mm threshold.
\item \textbf{Generalisation.} The model is assessed on the existing network, not on unseen stations, and non-training thresholds are queryable but not separately validated.
\item \textbf{Dependence.} Adjacent windows and nearby stations are strongly dependent, so no independent-sample confidence intervals are claimed.
\item \textbf{Inputs.} No radar, wind or neighbouring-station features are used; a convective system approaching from an adjacent station is invisible to the model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Conclusion}
This project built a complete Spark pipeline over 48.5 million rainfall observations and used it to forecast thirty-minute exceedance probabilities at four thresholds. Chronological selection across eleven candidates chose gradient-boosted trees at 60 iterations and depth 5, which reached a macro Brier score of 0.01461 on the held-out years 2023--2024, a 40.92\,\% improvement over a frequency baseline.

For the exploratory task, the yearly record shows variability rather than a trend. Across 2017--2024 there is no consistent rise or fall in rainfall. Instead 2019 stands out as dry, through a sharp fall in the number of wet days rather than in rain intensity, while 2021 and 2022 brought the heaviest rain. Even the seasonal cycle shifts: in 2020 and 2021 the middle of the year was wetter than the turn of the year. These differences are robust to the changing station network, but eight years are too few to call any of them a trend.

The most informative result is not the winning score but the ablation. Station identity, location, hour and season together perform near the frequency baseline in macro Brier; adding the current reading and six lags to these features captures 98\,\% of the measured improvement. On a thirty-minute horizon, adding recent rainfall at a station supplies most of the improvement over contextual features alone.

Two caveats travel with that conclusion. The seasonal baseline B1 was no better than the global baseline B0, which is consistent with most of the Brier improvement coming from recent observations rather than from station or month averages. And performance degrades sharply with rarity: precision falls from 0.698 at 0\,mm to 0.373 at 10\,mm, so the model ranks risk well but should not be read as a warning system for heavy rain. Its 10\,mm predictions also overestimate the observed frequency on average in the lowest probability bin, which covers 99.2\,\% of origins, and require further calibration assessment before operational use.

Overall, these conclusions apply to the configuration tested: a single-machine Spark run, the existing station network and four thresholds, with the further caveats listed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. They do not extend to unseen stations, to real-time operation with publication delay, or to claims about long-term changes in Singapore's rainfall climate.
